Presenter notes
How to run it (about 8 minutes)
  1. Ask everyone to have paper ready. Don't tell them what the experiment is about.
  2. Test 1 · Watch the panel. Read the instruction aloud, then press Ready. The panel shows, the screen goes gray with a math question, then the panel comes back with one thing changed. This tests how an interruption wipes out your running picture of the plant (Bainbridge's “working storage”).
  3. On the “write it down” screen, everyone writes which part changed before anyone speaks. Press Reveal, count hands, enter the count and class size.
  4. Test 2 · Take control. The class is now the operator. Pressure drifts on its own; they shout “UP!” or “DOWN!” to keep it in the green band, and you click the buttons (or press ↑ ↓). Meanwhile, the tank level in the bottom-left corner drops slowly from 66% to 55%. There's no interruption.
  5. Ask what else changed besides pressure, reveal, and count hands again. The reveal also shows how long they kept pressure in the green.
  6. Compare: Test 1 shows interruptions erase what you knew; Test 2 shows being in control helps only for the variable you're controlling.
  7. Bonus: the Flicker loop alternates two panels with a blank between them until someone shouts out the change. This is the classic lab version.
  • Shortcuts: Esc returns here at any time; Space moves on from the “write it down” screen.
  • The results are a classroom illustration, not real data. Say so when you show them.

Results

Who noticed the change?

Share of the class that correctly named the change in each test. One classroom is a demonstration, not a study: treat any gap as something to discuss, not a finding.

Discussion prompts

The gray screen

In a real plant, or with an AI agent editing your code, what plays the role of the interruption? A coffee break, a tab switch, ten minutes of the agent working alone.

In control, but only of one thing

In Test 2 you kept pressure on target, yet missed the tank level slowly dropping. Bainbridge: operators notice the variables they control, “but may be equally poor at noticing changes on others.”

Designing for it

Her fix is better alarms and displays. What would a “changed since you last looked” display look like? Think of a git diff.
